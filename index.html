01 / SURVEY OVERVIEW

Trustworthiness under
Recursive Self-Improvement

Self-modification alone does not guarantee trustworthy improvement. Gains, constraints, and oversight must be reassessed as systems and their improvement mechanisms change.

Systems built around large models increasingly use experience and feedback to make persistent changes to models, prompts, memories, tools, and workflows. Recursive self-improvement (RSI) extends this process to inductive biases and to the mechanisms that govern future updates.

This survey presents an operational definition and process framework for RSI, and examines risks, safeguards, and evaluation evidence for individual updates, effects inherited by successor systems, and revisions to improvement mechanisms. It distinguishes direct RSI findings from evidence in related systems.

Reading guide · Manuscript §1 and §2.3 · Analytical roles describe what is assessed; they are not stages of the improvement process.

01

Improvement Validity

Whether reported gains are genuine, retained, and transferable under valid evaluation.

02

Whether behavioral and information protections remain effective as changes are inherited and reused.

03

Effective Oversight

Whether explanations, verification, and authorized intervention remain useful after recursive revision.

02 / PRELIMINARIES

The RSI Process

S1–S4 revise the policy h or inductive bias B. Recursive improvement (RI) applies the same stages to the bias update function U itself.

Manuscript §2.1 · Policy h, inductive bias B, and bias update function U have distinct roles. Improvement is an objective, not a guaranteed result.

OPERATIONAL DEFINITION

RSI is a process in which a learning system not only updates its policy using new experience but also autonomously updates its inductive bias. The mechanism that updates this bias is itself recursively revised, with the aim of sustained gains in task performance.

Policyht → ht+1
Inductive biasBt → Bt+1
Bias update functionUt → Ut+1
Accepted revisions are retained for subsequent cycles.

05 / EVALUATING TRUSTWORTHY RSI

Three Levels of
Trustworthiness Claims

Update, successor, and meta-improvement claims concern different objects and require different evidence. They are not additional stages of the RSI process.

WHAT THE EVIDENCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH

A successful update does not establish trustworthy recursive improvement.

Evidence must name the update, lineage, or updater tested, together with its task scope, assessment horizon, and untested conditions. At the meta-improvement level, both the quality of later updates and the validity of their checks and controls require reassessment.

07 / APPLICATIONS AND OPEN PROBLEMS

Open Problems
and Future Directions

Application context changes the evidence required: what the system can modify, who controls evaluation feedback, and what persists after acceptance.

EDITABLE SURFACE

What can the system change?

Persistent artifacts, system behavior and implementation, or the improvement mechanism itself.

INDEPENDENCE OF FEEDBACK

Who controls the test?

Self-authored evaluation, co-evolving evaluators, or external and protected evaluation.

CONSEQUENCES OF COMMITMENT

What happens after acceptance?

Reversible software changes, persistent derived state, or consequential real-world actions.

Manuscript §8.3 · The seven directions below preserve the manuscript’s headings; descriptions are condensed for the website.

01Theoretical Foundations of Trustworthiness under Recursive Change+

Develop accounts of how trustworthiness requirements change when the policy, inductive bias, and bias update function evolve together.

02Verifying Self-Modifying Improvement Mechanisms+

Assess whether verification assumptions, acceptance rules, and authorized controls remain valid when the mechanisms producing future updates are revised.

03Long-Horizon Risk Dynamics and Adaptive Mitigation+

Study how risks persist, interact, or change across successor systems, and evaluate mitigation over longer improvement trajectories.

04Recursive Improvement of Trustworthiness Mechanisms+

Examine revisions to evaluators, monitors, and safeguards themselves, with independent evidence of their effects on subsequent updates.

05From Test-Time Scaling to Test-Time Controlling+

Investigate how runtime control and intervention can accompany additional test-time computation and adaptation.

06Fundamental Trade-offs and Limits of Trustworthy RSI+

Characterize the limits and trade-offs among improvement, constraint preservation, verification cost, and effective oversight.

07Domain-Specific and Multi-Stakeholder Trustworthiness+

Adapt evaluation and governance to the requirements of different domains, affected populations, and stakeholders.
